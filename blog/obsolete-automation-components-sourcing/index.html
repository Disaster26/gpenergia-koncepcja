How to source obsolete automation components safely (and avoid counterfeits)

5 November 2026 · 6 min read · GP ENERGIA

A line stops because one I/O module failed. The module was discontinued years ago, the maintenance stock is empty and the plant manager wants a date. This is one of the most common requests we get, and also one of the riskiest purchases in industrial automation: the part is scarce, the pressure is high, and that is exactly the situation counterfeiters look for.

Here is how we approach it, step by step.

1. Identify the part exactly

"S7-300 CPU" is not a part number. Before you ask anyone for a price, collect:

  • the full order code from the label (for Siemens, all 16 characters, such as 6ES7315-2EH14-0AB0),
  • the hardware revision and firmware version - Siemens prints a functional state (FS), Allen-Bradley uses a series letter. A different revision can behave differently or refuse to run your program,
  • a photo of the label and of the module in the rack,
  • how many you need now and how many you want on the shelf.

Ten minutes spent here saves days of back-and-forth and stops you from buying a module that looks right but is not.

2. Check where the product is in its lifecycle

Manufacturers publish lifecycle status. Take the Siemens SIMATIC S7-300 as an example: Siemens announced the phase-out from 1 October 2023, standard deliveries ended on 1 October 2025, and spare parts are typically available for about ten more years, until around 2033. So "discontinued" does not always mean "unavailable" - in the spare-parts phase you can still buy new parts, but stock gets thinner and prices tend to rise.

Knowing the phase tells you how hard to look, what to pay and whether it is time to plan a migration instead of buying one more module.

3. Know your sources and what each one gives you

  • Authorized distribution and the manufacturer's spare-parts service. Best traceability and full warranty. Often the first choice while the part is still in the spare-parts phase.
  • Remaining stock at other European distributors. Many old parts sit in a warehouse in another country. Searching across borders is a large part of our daily work.
  • Independent suppliers and surplus stock. Useful when official channels are empty. Quality varies widely, so this is where you need the checks below.
  • Used or refurbished parts. Sometimes the only option. Insist on knowing exactly what was tested and what warranty the seller gives.

Be clear about condition. "New in sealed original box", "new, opened box", "used, tested" and "refurbished" are four different products at four different prices. Get the condition in writing on the quote.

4. Watch for counterfeit warning signs

Counterfeit and relabelled automation parts are a real problem. Rockwell Automation, for example, states that its warranty only covers products bought from an authorized source, that products from unauthorized sellers come without a licence for its firmware and software, and it lists red flags such as unsolicited offers and sellers outside your region.

Practical warning signs we see:

  • a price far below every other offer,
  • stock of a scarce part available in large quantities "immediately",
  • marketplace listings with stock photos instead of photos of the actual item,
  • labels with the wrong font, blurred print, missing or mismatched serial numbers or date codes,
  • no original packaging, or packaging that does not match the product generation,
  • a seller who will not say where the part came from.

5. Verify before you install

  • Ask for photos of the actual item, label and packaging, before shipment.
  • Ask for traceability: where the part came from and in what condition.
  • Compare the label with a known genuine unit from your own plant.
  • Test on a bench before it goes into a running system, especially for CPUs, safety relays and communication modules.
  • Ask your supplier how they avoid counterfeits. In the electronics trade, the SAE AS6081 standard describes counterfeit-avoidance practices for distributors, and it is a good checklist even if your supplier is not certified to it.

If any of this is refused, walk away. An extra week is cheaper than a counterfeit CPU in a running line.

6. Know when to stop buying the original

Every obsolete part has a point where buying it again costs more than replacing it. Signs you have reached it: prices rising with every order, only used stock left, the same failure repeating. At that point, a compatible alternative or a planned migration is the safer choice. IEC 62402 (obsolescence management) gives a framework for planning this before a failure forces your hand.

When we propose an alternative, we send the documentation so your engineer can check dimensions, functions and wiring before you order. In one project, a general contractor upgrading BMS and fire alarm systems from 2005-2010 found that the design relied on relays, detectors and control panels no longer made. Together with an integrator, we proposed a new system concept that was technically compliant and cheaper than the original plan.

Frequently asked questions

Can I still buy new Siemens S7-300 parts?

Standard deliveries of the S7-300 ended on 1 October 2025. The range is now in the spare-parts phase, typically until around 2033, so many new parts can still be bought, but stock is shrinking and prices tend to rise.

How can I tell if a PLC module is counterfeit?

Look for a price far below market, labels with wrong fonts or mismatched serial numbers and date codes, missing original packaging, and a seller who cannot say where the part came from. Ask for photos of the actual item and test it on a bench before installing it.

Is a part from an independent supplier covered by the manufacturer's warranty?

Often not. Some manufacturers, such as Rockwell Automation, state that their warranty only covers products bought from authorized sources. Agree the warranty terms with your supplier before ordering.

What should I do when no original stock is left?

Choose a compatible alternative with documentation your engineer can check, or plan a migration to the current product range. Do not wait for the next failure to start.
